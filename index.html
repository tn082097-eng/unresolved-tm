The May 8, 2026 UAP Disclosure · A reader's anatomy

What the Pentagon's UFO files actually say.

On May 8, 2026, the U.S. government published 161 declassified files about pilot encounters with unidentified objects. Most coverage focused on the videos. This page reads what's inside the 41 mission reports that go with them — and finds a few things the official framing does not mention.

In plain words: real declassified documents, professionally handled, but two of the biggest aren't actually UFO reports, one mislabels its own location by half a world, four separate Pentagon declassification cycles were bundled into one publication, and eight numbered slots in the release were quietly left empty.

§ 00 / Start Here

What this is, in plain English.

Who made this
An independent reader who downloaded the Pentagon's release, ran the files through standard PDF tools, and wrote down what they found. No insider access. No claim to extraordinary technology. Just careful reading of public documents.

For decades, military pilots have filed reports when they see something in the sky they can't identify. Most of those reports stayed classified. On May 8, 2026, the U.S. government released a big batch of them to the public.

The release came with a slick website at war.gov/UFO, dramatic music in the videos, and language describing every case as an "Unresolved UAP" (UAP = "Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena," the official replacement for "UFO"). The press ran with that framing.

This page does something different: it reads the actual mission reports that go with the videos — the paperwork the pilots and intelligence operators filled out at the time. The reports tell a more complicated story than the headlines suggest.

What you'll find on this page

Two of the biggest documents in the release aren't UFO reports at all. They're a 1996 study on rocket booster failures and a Vandenberg Air Force Base launch summary from 1958–2000. Hundreds of pages of space-launch reference material, bundled in with no UAP content whatsoever.

One report has its location and year wrong on the title page. The file is labelled "Southern United States, 2020" but the contents are an F-16 fighter mission over Syria in March 2023. A reporter trusting the title would be off by half a planet and three years.

At least four separate declassification batches were merged into one release. Two USCENTCOM cycles from this year (one of which uses hidden renumbering — files numbered D49–D75 in the release were actually D27–D43 internally), plus a third USCENTCOM cycle from the prior fiscal year, plus a separate Joint Staff cycle. Two of the four were completed months earlier and stockpiled for this publication date.

Eight document slots in the middle (D66–D73) were left empty. Reserved for something that didn't make this release. Could be additional reports held back, or a future release. The metadata doesn't tell us which.

Glossary · jargon you'll see below

UAP Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena
The official term that replaced "UFO." Means anything in the sky (or sea) the military can't immediately identify — not necessarily extraterrestrial.
MISREP Mission Report
The standardized form a pilot or intel operator fills out after a flight. Most files in this release are MISREPs.
AARO All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office
The Pentagon office set up in 2022 to investigate UAP cases. They received the documents in this release.
MDR Mandatory Declassification Review
The legal process for un-classifying documents on request. The release is two MDR batches merged into one.
USCENTCOM U.S. Central Command
The military command responsible for the Middle East. Most reports in this release come from CENTCOM's area: Iraq, Syria, the Persian Gulf.
Dept. of War (DoW) formerly Dept. of Defense
Renamed by Trump in early 2026. The release was published under the new name. "DOW-UAP" is just the file prefix.
Reaper / MQ-9 remotely-piloted drone
The unmanned aircraft that recorded most of these sightings — it streams video back to ground operators in real time.
ISR Intelligence, Surveillance, Reconnaissance
The mission type for "go look at something and report back." Most UAP sightings in this release happened during routine ISR flights.
FMV Full Motion Video
The live video feed from the drone's camera. "Method of observation: FMV" means the operator saw it on screen, not out a window.
FL (Flight Level) altitude
Hundreds of feet above sea level. FL200 = 20,000 ft. FL600 = 60,000 ft (very high; spy-plane territory).
JBIG2 image-compression format
Used by office scanners to shrink black-and-white scans. Has a notorious bug where digits get swapped during compression (a "6" becomes an "8"). Affects 14 files here.
xref table PDF's internal table of contents
Every time a PDF is saved or modified, a new xref is added. Counting them tells you how many times the file was edited.
OCG Optional Content Group
PDF "layers" — like watermarks or headers that can be toggled on/off. Looked at as a possible redaction-hiding trick. Wasn't one.
Series 1 / Series 2 our shorthand
The two merged release batches. Series 1 = the 24 files with continuous IDs matching their filenames. Series 2 = the 17 files with renumbered IDs (the "hidden renumbering" finding).

§ 02 / Headline Findings

Three things the filenames don't say.

Method
Every PDF inspected via pdfinfo, exiftool, and qpdf. Title/Subject metadata compared against filename and rendered content. xref-revision counts and ModDate clustering used to identify processing batches.
01 / KEY FINDING

Two of the largest documents aren't UAP reports.

D48 (181 pages, 22 MB) is RTI Report 5180/77-43F: "Modeling Unlikely Space-Booster Failures in Risk Calculations," a 1996 contractor study for Vandenberg and Patrick AFB on Atlas / Titan / Delta booster reliability.

D49 (113 pages, 9 MB) is the 30th Space Wing's Vandenberg AFB Launch Summary, 1958–2000.

Together: 294 pages of space-launch reference material with zero UAP mentions, no USCENTCOM declassification stamps, and a 16-year temporal gap between D49's last entry (Feb 2000) and the earliest UAP report in this corpus (D55, Nov 2016).

A follow-up search of DTIC, RTI International's archives, NASA NTRS, archive.org, AARO's Volume 1 bibliography, and Google Books found no public version of either document anywhere. D48 carries a Distribution Statement B/C marking, meaning it was never intended for public release. The closest public Vandenberg launch history (archive.org) ends in 1996, not Feb 2000 — different document. Both D48 and D49 appear to be internal-distribution DoD reference material that the public has never seen before this release.

In plain English

Imagine someone hands you a folder labelled "UFO sightings" and inside are 39 actual sighting reports plus two thick reference books titled "a history of every rocket launch we did" and "why rockets sometimes fail." Those reference books aren't sightings — they're the kind of thing an investigator pulls off the shelf to check whether a reported "UFO" was actually a known rocket launch. We initially assumed they were public documents bundled in for transparency. They aren't. Both are internal DoD reference material that has never been publicly released anywhere we can find. So why are they in a UAP disclosure with their own DOW-UAP file numbers? The metadata doesn't tell us.

02 / KEY FINDING

The release bundles at least four separate declassification cycles.

Initial inspection found two merged MDR batches with hidden renumbering. Closer examination of the bottom-of-page MDR stamps (visible only when pages are rendered at 300 DPI) revealed two additional tracks:

  • USCENTCOM MDR 26-0038 → 26-0046 · 23 files (most Series 1) · declass May–Jun 2026
  • USCENTCOM MDR 26-0028 · 17 files (all Series 2, with hidden renumbering D27–D43 → D49–D75) · declass May 2026
  • USCENTCOM MDR 25-0104 · D23 only · declass 12 Sep 2025 — 8 months before release
  • Joint Staff JS-250710-TM8S / MDR 25-0094 → 25-0099 · D20 only · declass 8 Oct 2025 — 7 months before release · six consecutive case numbers, only D20 surfaces

Two of these tracks were completed months before the public release date and held for inclusion. This is more consistent with deliberate curation across multiple Pentagon offices than with a single contemporaneous declassification action.

In plain English

The May 8, 2026 release looks like one big drop, but it's actually at least four different declassification jobs bundled together. Two of them were finished months earlier (a separate USCENTCOM case in September 2025, and a Joint Staff case in October 2025) but held back for the May publication. So the timing of the release was chosen for outside reasons — political, PR, executive order, whatever — and the declassification work was stockpiled in advance.

03 / METADATA ERROR

D20's title is wrong on both location and year.

The internal Title reads "DOW-UAP-D20, Mission Report, Southern United States, 2020."

The actual content is an F-16CM 2-ship mission taking off from Prince Sultan Air Base, Saudi Arabia on 19 March 2023, transiting Iraq and Syria airspace under Operation Inherent Resolve, observing UAPs in the Eastern Syria Security Area.

Not Southern US. Not 2020. An analyst trusting the title would mis-locate the report by half a world and three years.

In plain English

The title page of file D20 says it's a 2020 sighting somewhere in the southern United States. The actual contents are a March 2023 fighter mission over Syria. If a journalist or researcher cited this report as "a domestic U.S. sighting from 2020," they'd be wrong on every count. A clerical error, probably — but a meaningful one if anyone takes the labels at face value.

§ 07 / Forensic Notes

What the file structure tells you.

Detail
Beyond the headlines: how the files were produced, what the encryption actually does (and doesn't), where compression introduces risk, and what was checked and ruled out.
01

The "no extract" encryption flag is advisory only.

All 41 files use AES-256 with permission flags blocking text extraction. pdftotext from poppler ignores the flag entirely and pulls the underlying text layer. For scanned files, that layer was added by Adobe Acrobat's Paper Capture OCR — invisible beneath the page image but fully readable. The encryption deters polite PDF readers; any forensic tool walks straight through it.

Plain English

The files are locked with strong encryption — but the lock is more of a "please don't" than an actual barrier. Free open-source tools read the text straight out. The interesting part is what's not there: redactions are baked in permanently, not hidden behind a removable layer. No one is going to "decrypt" the blacked-out sections.

02

Three production tiers, identified from Producer metadata.

Born-digital (blank Producer field, ~22 files): the MISREP form filled in digitally and exported. Paper Capture scans (Producer: Adobe Acrobat (32-bit) 26 Paper Capture Plug-in, ~17 files): physical paper scanned and OCR'd by Acrobat. Pristine (no post-creation edits, 2 files — D42 and D48): created once, never modified.

Plain English

The files came from three different production paths. Some operators typed their reports straight into a digital form. Others printed and rescanned theirs. A couple were created once and never touched again. This matters because scanned-and-OCR'd reports can introduce errors — see the next item.

03

JBIG2 compression risk: checked three ways, all clean.

The Xerox JBIG2 character-substitution bug (notably 6↔8 swaps) affects 10 files (revised down from an earlier count of 14 — pdfimages -list showed that D23, D61, D62, D64 use JPEG/JPX rather than JBIG2). Actual JBIG2 set: D14 D16 D18 D19 D20 D33 D35 D38 D48 D49. Every suspect numeric value was cross-checked via three independent extraction methods: (1) pdftotext pulling Adobe's invisible OCR layer, (2) tesseract OCR on rendered images, (3) re-rendering specific pages at 300 DPI and re-OCRing. All three methods agreed on every value.

Verdicts: D20 FL600+ is a real operator estimate (the "+" suffix is operator's explicit "or higher" notation). D62 FL040 is the scanned value as-is, but D62 doesn't use JBIG2 anyway. D23 58 KIAS at FL230 appears to be a partial-redaction artifact (likely 158 KIAS with leading "1" inside a redaction box). All other altitudes and speeds are normal patrol values.

Plain English

The famous Xerox digit-swap bug could have corrupted numbers in 14 of the files — coordinates, altitudes, times. I checked the suspicious-looking ones by reading them three different ways and comparing. All three readings agreed. That makes substitution unlikely. D20's "objects at 60,000+ ft" is what the operator wrote down (with an explicit "estimate or higher" mark). D62's "4,000 ft" is also what's actually on the page. The numbers in this release appear trustworthy.

03a

D20 was declassified through Joint Staff, not USCENTCOM — seven months earlier.

While verifying D20's flight-level values by rendering page 6 at 300 DPI, the bottom-of-page MDR markings became readable: MDR 25-0094 thru MDR 25-0099 / JS-250710-TM8S, declassified 8 October 2025. The "JS" prefix is Joint Staff, not USCENTCOM. D20 came through a separate declassification channel and was bundled into the May 2026 batch despite its prior release.

Plain English

The May 8, 2026 release is presented as a single USCENTCOM declassification, but D20 was actually declassified months earlier by a different Pentagon office (the Joint Staff). It got rolled into this release alongside the USCENTCOM material. So the corpus isn't a single declassification — it's a curated bundle from multiple tracks, with at least one document predating the public release by seven months. May be findable elsewhere in Joint Staff FOIA reading rooms.

03c

D23 came through USCENTCOM MDR 25-0104 — and was scanned in color, not black-and-white.

Like D20, D23 carries its own distinct MDR provenance: bottom-of-page stamps read USCENTCOM MDR 25-0104 declassified 12 September 2025. Eight months before the May 2026 release. This is a USCENTCOM MDR but from the prior fiscal year (FY25), not the FY26 cycle the rest of the corpus uses.

D23 is also the only file in the corpus stored as RGB JPEG images at 144 DPI (one full-page color image per page) instead of the standard 1-bit JBIG2 scans. The text content is a normal MQ-9 mission report (UAE, October 2023); the unusual format probably exists to preserve the red declassification stamps and other color markings. Initial analysis miscounted D23 as a JBIG2 file — it isn't, and the JBIG2 risk count drops from 14 to 10 as a result.

Plain English

D23 is the second file (after D20) where the bottom-of-page stamps reveal it came through a separate declassification track months before the public release. The May 2026 release isn't one single declassification — it's a curated bundle from at least four separate Pentagon processes, with the publication date chosen for outside reasons and the declassification work stockpiled.

03b

D20's crew explicitly tested whether the bright objects were stars. They weren't.

Buried in D20's GENTEXT/UAP block: "ANALYST COMMENT: [REDACTED] FLT COMPARED TARGETING POD BETWEEN POSS UAP AND STAR. RESULTS WERE DIFFERENT." The flight crew (an F-16CM two-ship) deliberately compared the targeting pod's signature on a candidate UAP against a known star. They did not match. This is the most common prosaic explanation for high-altitude visual UAP sightings, and the operators ruled it out at the time.

Plain English

Most "UFOs" seen at high altitude on infrared sensors turn out to be stars or satellites. D20's pilots tested for exactly that by pointing the targeting pod at a star and comparing. The signatures didn't match. So D20 isn't a careless report — it's an operator-investigated case where the obvious prosaic explanation was actively ruled out by the crew at the time of the observation.

04

Series 1 went through three post-creation revisions. Series 2, one.

Series 1 files contain four %%EOF markers each: skeleton form → bulk content add → small modification → final encryption. Series 2 files have two markers (skeleton → final). Earlier xref tables were checked for recoverable pre-redaction content; they contain only empty form skeletons. Redactions were baked into the bulk content addition, not applied as later overlays.

Plain English

PDFs keep a small history of how they were saved over time. We checked whether any of that history secretly preserved pre-redaction text (the stuff behind the black bars). It doesn't — the earlier saves are just empty form templates. Whoever did the redactions did them properly.

05

Encryption was applied as two batches on consecutive evenings.

All Series 2 files (D49–D75) have ModDate timestamps clustered between 20:33 and 20:38 EDT on May 6, 2026. All Series 1 files (D3–D48) cluster between 15:43 and 16:44 EDT on May 7, 2026. Within each batch, timestamps run in alphabetical filename order at ~20-second intervals — a single sequential script. Series 2 was finalized first, despite carrying the lower internal IDs.

Plain English

The 41 files weren't finalized one by one over weeks — they were processed by a script in two big batches, on two consecutive evenings, just before the May 8 publication. The "renumbered" batch (Series 2) was actually processed first, even though it ended up with higher numbers in the final release. The reshuffling happened between the two batches.

06

D74 and D75 sat encrypted for eleven months.

Both have CreationDate May 16, 2025 but ModDate May 6, 2026. They're also the only two Series 2 files that retained the original MISREP form's Optional Content Group layers — the production prototypes that the rest of the batch was modelled on.

Plain English

Two files were prepared in May 2025 — almost a year before the release — and then sat around until the May 2026 publication. They also look like the "template" files that the rest of the batch was modelled on. Whatever review or decision kept them on hold for 11 months happened off-screen; the metadata only shows the dates.

07

D49 was created February 7, 2024 — 14 months before any other Series 2 file.

The Vandenberg launch summary was prepared well ahead of the rest of the batch. The timing aligns roughly with AARO's Historical Record Report Volume 1 (March 2024) — possibly compiled as supporting evidence for that effort.

Plain English

D49 (the Vandenberg launch summary — one of the two non-UAP reference docs) is the oldest modern file in the batch by more than a year. The timing roughly matches when AARO published its big historical UAP report in March 2024, suggesting D49 may have been compiled as background research for that report, not for this release.

08

Things checked and ruled out.

D16's /JS count was random bytes inside an encrypted stream, not actual JavaScript. D48's /AA was the same false positive. OCG layers in D10 / D74 / D75 are functional template watermarks set to /ON for all events, not redaction-hiding vectors. Pre-redaction content is not recoverable from earlier xref tables — those tables hold empty form skeletons only.

Plain English

Some things looked suspicious at first glance and turned out to be nothing: no embedded scripts, no hidden auto-actions, no removable redaction overlays, no recoverable original text behind the black bars. Whoever prepared the files did the redactions correctly. Saying so explicitly because "didn't find anything" is also a finding.

§ 08 / Bottom Line

What this release actually establishes.

The Department of War published 161 real declassified files on May 8, 2026, in response to a real Trump directive of February 19, 2026. The technical handling — image-only PDFs, scrubbed EXIF, zeroed MP4 timestamps — is professional and conservative. There is no forensic slip-up exposing under-redaction.

The framing is doing more work than the contents. The war.gov UI presents a uniform Unresolved UAP wall of mystery. The DVIDS captions, which DoD's publication conventions require, reveal that two-thirds of the videos in the tranche are either identified by the original operator as something known (a Russian fighter, a possible missile, four possible birds), explicitly flagged by the operator as a sensor artifact (halo, glare, single-sensor detection, possible water reflection), or have no original-observer description at all.

The mission reports — now text-extracted — show several more with prosaic explanations baked in: IR lens flares during weapons employment, targets becoming dim and disappearing on a targeting pod, operator-doubted reflections off water. The D33 / PR-34 Greece case (90-degree turns at ~80 mph above the ocean) remains one of the few entries without a ready explanation in its own report.

Two further things the metadata makes clear: the 41 D## files are not all UAP reports — D48 and D49 are space-launch reference material with zero UAP content, included as analyst context for cases not present in this release. And the release was assembled from two separate MDR cycles merged with hidden renumbering, with eight reserved external slots (D66–D73) sitting empty for a future cycle.

The release is real. The mystery framing is doing a lot of work. And the story of what's in this batch is also a story of what's been deferred.
